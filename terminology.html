Doctrinal disputes that turned on words

Terms, pronunciations, what was at stake, and whether it was ever settled.

325–381 Settled

homoousioshoh-moh-OO-see-oshomoiousioshoh-moy-OO-see-oshomoioshoh-MOY-os

Homoousios means of the same substance. Homoiousios means of like substance. Homoios means only similar. The first two differ by a single letter in Greek.

What the letter decides is which side of one line Christ falls on: the line between God and everything God made. Of the same substance puts the Son on God's side, uncreated and divine exactly as the Father is. Of like substance puts him on the other side, the highest of creatures but a creature still.

Nothing small follows from that. It settles whether the incarnation is God entering his own creation or God sending someone else, whether prayer to Christ is worship or idolatry, and whether Christians hold to one God or to two unequal ones.

Arius, a senior priest in Alexandria, taught that the Son was the first and greatest of God's creatures, made out of nothing before time began, so there was a point at which the Son did not exist. Athanasius, then a young deacon serving Alexandria's bishop, answered that a created savior cannot save.

Figures

Athanasius, Arius, Basil of Ancyra

Resolution

Nicaea adopted the first term; contested for two generations, ratified at Constantinople 381. Arian belief survived among Germanic peoples into the 7th century.

c. 360–381 Settled

ousiaOO-see-ahhypostasishy-POSS-tuh-sis

In ordinary Greek ousia and hypostasis both meant something close to being, or substance. They were near enough to interchangeable that a writer could reach for either one.

Nicaea made that a problem. The council appended a short list of condemnations to its creed, and one of them condemned anyone who said the Son came from a different hypostasis or ousia than the Father — naming the two words side by side as though they were one and the same.

So the church had two words carrying a single idea between them, at the exact moment it needed language for something harder: that God is one in a certain respect and three in another. Say three hypostaseis and, on the council's own wording, you had said three ousiai — three Gods.

The Cappadocians — Basil of Caesarea, his brother Gregory of Nyssa, and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus, three bishops from a province of central Asia Minor — broke the tie by giving each word a separate job. Ousia answers what God is, and there is one of those. Hypostasis answers who God is, and there are three.

Figures

Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa

Resolution

Became the standard formula. Latin rendered hypostasis as persona, because its literal cognate substantia meant the wrong thing.

431 Partly settled

theotokosthee-oh-TOH-koschristotokoskris-toh-TOH-kos

Theotokos means God-bearer and christotokos means Christ-bearer. Both were proposed as the proper title for Mary. Nestorius, the patriarch of Constantinople, preferred Christ-bearer, holding that Mary bore the man Jesus, in whom God dwelt, rather than the eternal God himself.

Cyril, patriarch of Alexandria and the man leading the case against him, answered that a mother bears a person and not a nature, and the person she bore was God; deny the title and the child born was not God at all.

Figures

Cyril of Alexandria, Nestorius

Resolution

Ephesus affirmed God-bearer and deposed Nestorius. The Church of the East never accepted it and remains separate, though a 1994 joint declaration with Rome found the underlying faith the same.

451–553 Unresolved

physisFY-sismia physis sesarkomeneMEE-ah FY-sis seh-sar-koh-MEH-nee

Physis means nature: what a thing is, as distinct from who it is. A human nature is everything it takes to be human, a body and a mind and a will. A divine nature is everything it takes to be God.

The Council of Chalcedon held that Christ is one person in two natures, fully God and at the same time a complete human being, the two neither blurred into each other nor held apart.

Its opponents argued from a different instinct: natures do not float free of the things that have them. Every nature is some particular thing's nature, and Christ is one particular thing, not two. Count two natures in him and you have begun counting two beings. So at the incarnation, they held, the divine and the human must have joined into a single nature, neither one thinning the other out.

Both sides quoted the same words of Cyril of Alexandria: mia physis sesarkomene, one incarnate nature of God the Word. Cyril had died in 444, seven years before the council met, and was never asked which of the two readings he meant.

Figures

Chalcedon, Dioscorus, Severus of Antioch

Resolution

Permanent schism with the Oriental Orthodox. Joint declarations from 1973 onward concluded the difference is largely terminological, but communion has not been restored.

c. 500

apophaticap-oh-FAT-ikkataphatickat-uh-FAT-ik

Kataphatic means speaking about God by affirming things, and apophatic means speaking about God by denying them. The problem behind both is that every word we have was learned from creatures.

So the kataphatic approach says what scripture and creation permit, that God is good and wise and powerful, and the apophatic approach then takes each of those back, holding that God is not good in any sense we can grasp, not one being among others, not even existent in the way ordinary things are. These are two methods rather than two parties, and most theologians use both.

Figures

Pseudo-Dionysius

Resolution

Both retained as complementary. The East leans harder on denial, and Gregory Palamas built directly on it eight centuries later.

c. 645 Unresolved

ekporeusisek-por-YOO-sisprocessiopro-CHESS-ee-oh

Ekporeusis is the Greek word the creed uses for the Spirit's coming forth from the Father, and processio is the Latin word used to translate it. They do not mean quite the same thing. The Greek word is reserved for coming forth from an ultimate origin, while the Latin covers any coming-forth, including one that passes through an intermediary.

So when Latins said the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, Greeks heard a claim that the Trinity has two first causes. Maximus the Confessor, a Greek monk who had lived for years in the Latin West, explained that the Latins meant the Son as a channel and not as a source.

Figures

Maximus the Confessor, later Photius

Resolution

Rome's 1995 clarification affirmed the Father as sole source, but the clause stays in the Western creed. Anglican bishops recommended dropping the clause at the Lambeth Conferences of 1978 and 1988, but most Anglican churches still say it.

649–681 Settled

energeiaen-ER-gay-ahthelemaTHEH-lay-mah

Thelema means will, and energeia means operation or activity, what a nature actually does. The question was whether willing belongs to a nature or to a person. The emperor's party in Constantinople proposed that Christ has only one will, offered as a compromise to win the miaphysite provinces back.

Maximus, a monk holding no rank in the hierarchy, objected that if Christ has no human will, then his human nature is missing something every other human being has.

He had a particular scene in mind. On the night before his execution, in the garden of Gethsemane, Christ asks that the cup pass from him and then gives way: not my will, but yours. Two wills are audible there, one asking and one being deferred to. Allow Christ only one, and there is no second will in the garden to submit, and the prayer is theater.

Figures

Maximus the Confessor, Sergius of Constantinople

Resolution

Third Constantinople defined two wills and two operations. Maximus, mutilated and exiled, died in 662 and was vindicated nineteen years later.

1215–1274 Settled for Rome only

substantiasub-STAHN-tsee-ahaccidentiaahk-chee-DEN-tsee-ah

Substantia means what a thing fundamentally is, and accidentia means the properties you can see, taste, and weigh. The distinction comes from Aristotle.

Transubstantiation holds that at the consecration the substance of the bread becomes the body of Christ while the accidents remain those of bread. The doctrine depends entirely on the distinction being real, since otherwise the claim is either about nothing observable or plainly false.

Figures

Fourth Lateran Council, Aquinas

Resolution

Trent affirmed transubstantiation as the apt term in 1551. Rejected by every Reformation tradition.

c. 1270–1300 Never adjudicated

analogia entisah-nah-LOH-gee-ah EN-teesunivocatio entisoo-nee-voh-KAHT-see-oh EN-tees

Analogia entis means analogy of being and univocatio entis means univocity of being. Both are answers to a question about the word exists. When you say that God exists and that a chair exists, is the word doing the same job in both sentences?

Thomas Aquinas, a thirteenth-century Dominican friar, said no, only a proportional likeness, because God's existing is not one instance of a general category that also contains chairs. Duns Scotus, a Franciscan of the generation after him, said that if the word changes meaning entirely, the sentence conveys nothing and no argument about God can be valid.

Figures

Aquinas, Duns Scotus; later Przywara, Barth

Resolution

No council ruled. Still live: Karl Barth, the Swiss Reformed theologian, named analogy of being in 1932 as the one thing keeping him from Rome.

1341–1351 Settled for the East

ousiaOO-see-ahenergeiaien-ER-gay-eye

Ousia here means God's essence, God as he is in himself, and energeiai means God's energies, his activity as it reaches outward into the world. Monks on Mount Athos claimed to see the uncreated light of the Transfiguration during prayer.

Gregory Palamas, a monk of Athos and later archbishop of Thessalonica, defended them by arguing that what they encounter is the energies, which are genuinely God and not a created substitute, while the essence remains permanently beyond reach. Barlaam of Calabria, a Greek-speaking scholar from southern Italy, answered that this divides God into a knowable part and an unknowable part, which gives you either two gods or none.

Figures

Gregory Palamas, Barlaam of Calabria

Resolution

Three Constantinopolitan councils upheld Palamas, and the distinction is now binding Orthodox doctrine. Never received in the West.

1529 Failed at the time

estestsignificatsig-NIF-ee-kaht

Est is the Latin word for is, and significat is the Latin word for signifies. The argument was over which one Jesus meant when he said “this is my body” at the Last Supper.

Martin Luther held the verb literal and would not move off it. Huldrych Zwingli, who was leading the reform in Zurich, read it as a figure of speech, the way “I am the vine” is a figure of speech, and argued that a body seated at God's right hand cannot also be present on ten thousand altars.

Figures

Luther, Zwingli

Resolution

Marburg agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles and broke on this one, splitting Lutheran from Reformed. The Leuenberg Agreement of 1973 finally restored communion in Europe.

1941–1980 Open

mythosMY-thoskerygmakeh-RIG-muh

Mythos means the picture of the world the Christian message arrived wrapped in, with a heaven overhead, an underworld below, and demons moving through the middle. Kerygma means the proclamation itself, the thing being announced. Rudolf Bultmann, a German New Testament scholar, argued that a modern person cannot believe the first and that it has to be stripped away to reach the second.

The question that followed is blunter. When a creed says that God made heaven and earth, is that a report about how things actually are, or is it a declaration of where the speaker stands?

Figures

Bultmann, Flew, D. Z. Phillips, Cupitt

Resolution

No institutional adjudication, and none likely. Churches have mostly declined to rule on what their own language is doing.

Two caveats. The story of Luther chalking the words on the table at Marburg comes from later accounts rather than the contemporary record, and is best treated as tradition. And the phrase both sides quoted at Chalcedon, which Cyril believed came from Athanasius, in fact originates with Apollinarius, a bishop condemned in 381 for teaching that Christ had no human mind.
