The branching

Every family below traces back to one body. The scale is even from AD 30 to today.

The 1054 date is a convenience. Rome and Constantinople drifted apart over centuries, and the mutual excommunications issued that year were lifted in 1965. The sack of Constantinople by Western crusaders in 1204 did more lasting damage than the paperwork did.

The five main families

The early church

c. 30 – 451

Christianity begins as a movement inside Judaism among followers of Jesus of Nazareth, executed in Jerusalem around AD 30. It spreads across the Roman Empire and past its eastern edge, and the emperor Constantine legalizes it in 313.

Bishops meeting at Nicaea in 325 and Constantinople in 381 produce the Nicene Creed, the statement of belief still recited in most of the churches below. By 451 the church is organized around five senior sees: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. Every family below claims descent from this one body.

Church of the East

Separates: 424 – 431

Centered in Persia, outside Roman rule, and self-governing from 424. It rejected the Council of Ephesus of 431, which condemned Nestorius, the patriarch of Constantinople, for so separating the divine and the human in Christ that Mary should not be called Mother of God.

This church holds that Christ has two clearly distinct natures, one divine and one human. Missionaries carried it along the Silk Road to India and China.

Today

Assyrian Church of the East. Under 1 million.

Oriental Orthodox

Separates: 451

The Council of Chalcedon in 451 defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human. These churches rejected that wording. They hold that the divine and human join into one united nature, a position called miaphysite, from the Greek for one nature.

Joint statements with Rome and with Eastern Orthodoxy since the 1970s conclude that the two sides mean much the same thing in different vocabularies.

Today

Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Malankara. About 50 million.

Eastern Orthodox

Separates: 1054, then 1204

The Greek-speaking East and the Latin-speaking West drifted apart over language, politics, and two disputes. The first is whether the pope holds authority over all other bishops. The second is the filioque, a Western addition of the words and the Son to the creed's line on where the Holy Spirit comes from.

Orthodoxy rejects both. Authority rests with councils of bishops and with self-governing national churches, each under its own patriarch.

Today

Greek, Russian, Serbian, Romanian, Antiochian. About 220 million.

Roman Catholic

Distinct: 1054 onward

The Western church, led by the bishop of Rome, the pope, who holds direct authority over every Catholic bishop. It recognizes seven sacraments, teaches purgatory as a state of purification after death, and keeps a formal teaching office that can settle doctrine.

The Council of Trent, 1545–63, set its answer to the Reformation. The Second Vatican Council, 1962–65, opened worship to local languages and to lay participation.

Today

Latin Church plus 23 Eastern Catholic churches. 1.3–1.4 billion.

Protestant

Separates: 1517 onward

Began when Martin Luther, a German friar and university professor, attacked the sale of indulgences, payments said to reduce punishment after death.

The churches that followed hold that Scripture outranks church tradition, that God accepts people through faith rather than earned merit, and that only baptism and communion count as sacraments. Protestantism is not one church but a family that keeps subdividing.

Today

Lutheran, Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal. About 1 billion.

The Protestant branch

Protestantism is not one church but a family that keeps subdividing.

Baptists and Quakers grew out of English Separatism, which was Reformed in theology, and Baptists also drew on Mennonite practice in Amsterdam. Pentecostalism came through the Holiness movement, which was Methodist. Restorationist and Adventist bodies drew on several streams at once, so they branch from the Protestant trunk rather than from any single parent.

Lutheran

1517 – 1530

Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar teaching at Wittenberg, posted 95 arguments against indulgences on October 31, 1517. He held that Scripture alone binds the conscience and that God accepts people through faith, not merit. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 set out the position. Lutheran churches keep liturgy, vestments, and the belief that Christ is truly present in the bread and wine.

Reformed

1519 – 1536

Huldrych Zwingli began reforming Zurich in 1519. John Calvin, a French lawyer living in exile in Geneva, published the Institutes of the Christian Religion in 1536. Both stripped worship back to preaching and Scripture and removed images from churches. Calvin taught that God elects, or chooses, who is saved. Boards of elders govern the churches; there are no bishops.

Anglican

1534 – 1571

Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534 to obtain an annulment, not over doctrine. Thomas Cranmer, his archbishop, supplied the theology through the Book of Common Prayer of 1549, which gave English worship a single form still in use. The Thirty-Nine Articles followed in 1571. Bishops and sacraments remain, and unity rests on praying together rather than on signing a doctrinal statement.

Radical Reformation (Anabaptist)

1525

In January 1525 a group in Zurich baptized one another as adults, arguing that infant baptism does not count because faith cannot be inherited. Opponents called them Anabaptists, meaning rebaptizers. They held that the church is a voluntary body of believers that the state has no business running, and many refused oaths and military service.

Catholic and Protestant authorities executed them in large numbers. Their descendants include the Mennonites, named for the Dutch leader Menno Simons, and the Amish, who separated in 1693 under Jakob Ammann.

Baptist

1609

John Smyth, an English separatist exiled in Amsterdam, baptized himself and his congregation as believing adults in 1609. Baptists hold that baptism follows a personal decision, that each congregation governs itself, and that no creed binds it.

Congregational and Quaker

1580s and 1652

English separatists under Robert Browne rejected bishops outright from the 1580s; their descendants founded Plymouth Colony. George Fox's Quakers, from 1652, went further and dropped clergy and sacraments, meeting in silence until someone speaks.

Methodist

1738 – 1795

John Wesley, an Anglican priest, led a revival built on personal holiness, lay preaching, and hymns written largely by his brother Charles. American Methodists organized separately in 1784, the British in 1795, after Wesley's death in 1791.

Episcopal Church (US)

1784 – 1789

After independence, American Anglicans could no longer swear loyalty to the British crown. Samuel Seabury was consecrated bishop in Scotland in 1784, and the church adopted a constitution and its own prayer book at the 1789 General Convention.

Adventist and Restorationist

1832 – 1863

Movements that set aside later church history and tried to rebuild the first-century church from Scripture alone. The Stone-Campbell churches merged in 1832. Seventh-day Adventists, who keep Saturday as the Sabbath, organized in 1863.

Pentecostal

1901 – 1906

Began with students in Topeka, Kansas, in 1901 and spread from William Seymour's Azusa Street revival in Los Angeles in 1906. Teaches a second experience after conversion, baptism in the Holy Spirit, marked by speaking in tongues and by healing.

Latter-day Saints, founded in 1830, and Jehovah's Witnesses, organized from the 1870s, are usually charted apart from Protestantism. Both reject the doctrine of the Trinity set out at Nicaea, which every family above shares.

The seven sacraments are baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, ordination, and marriage. Most Protestant churches count only baptism and the Eucharist, on the grounds that Scripture records Jesus instituting those two.

Key dates

325

Council of Nicaea

Condemns Arius, who taught that the Son was created by the Father rather than eternal with him.
381

Council of Constantinople

Adds the section on the Holy Spirit and completes the creed still recited in most of these churches.
431

Council of Ephesus

Condemns Nestorius over how the divine and human join in Christ. The Church of the East does not follow.
451

Council of Chalcedon

Defines Christ as one person in two natures. The Oriental Orthodox reject the wording and separate.
787

Second Council of Nicaea

Ends the first campaign against religious images. The last council both East and West accept.
1054

Mutual excommunications

Envoys of Pope Leo IX and Patriarch Michael Cerularius excommunicate each other in Constantinople.
1204

Sack of Constantinople

Crusaders bound for Egypt take the city instead and install a Latin ruler for nearly sixty years.
1378–1417

Western Schism

Rival popes in Rome and Avignon claim the office at once, discrediting the papacy and inviting reform.
1517

Luther's 95 Theses

Academic objections to the sale of indulgences circulate in print and become a rupture within months.
1525

Zurich adult baptisms

Adults are rebaptized in Zurich, beginning the Radical Reformation and its rejection of state churches.
1534

Act of Supremacy

Parliament makes Henry VIII head of the English church, ending papal jurisdiction in England.
1545–1563

Council of Trent

Rome defines its doctrine against the Reformers and overhauls the training and conduct of clergy.
1549

Book of Common Prayer

Thomas Cranmer gives English worship one vernacular form, still the basis of Anglican liturgy.
1609

First Baptist congregation

John Smyth baptizes believing adults in Amsterdam, giving believer's baptism organized form.
1738

Wesley's conversion

John Wesley's experience at a London meeting begins the Methodist movement inside Anglicanism.
1789

Episcopal Church constituted

American Anglicans adopt a constitution and their own prayer book after independence.
1870

First Vatican Council

Defines that the pope teaches without error when ruling formally on faith and morals.
1906

Azusa Street revival

William Seymour's revival in Los Angeles turns Pentecostalism into a global movement.
1962–1965

Second Vatican Council

Mass in local languages, openness to other churches, and a larger role for lay people.
1965

Excommunications lifted

Paul VI and Athenagoras revoke the mutual condemnations of 1054.

The creed. The text called the Nicene Creed is traditionally credited to the councils of 325 and 381, and this page follows that convention. The full text first appears in the record at Chalcedon in 451, so the attribution to 381 is traditional rather than documented.

Population figures. The totals are approximate and depend on how you count. Baptismal registers favor Catholic and Orthodox numbers; survey self-identification favors Protestant ones. The World Christian Database at Gordon-Conwell, 2025, gives 1.27 billion Catholics, 639 million Protestants, 426 million Independents, and 292 million Orthodox, that last figure combining Eastern and Oriental. The Vatican's 2025 yearbook reports 1.406 billion baptized Catholics through 2023. Pew Research Center puts Protestants, broadly defined, at roughly 37 percent of all Christians.

Eastern Catholic churches. These use Eastern liturgies while remaining in communion with Rome, and belong on the Catholic branch rather than the Orthodox one. Some came from reunions: the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church traces to the Union of Brest in 1596, the largest of them. The Maronite Church of Lebanon has no history of separation from Rome at all.
