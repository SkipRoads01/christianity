Lines of descent

East and West did not separate in 1054. They had been drifting apart for six centuries or more: Theodosius split the empire in two in 395, and Rome and Constantinople had already excommunicated each other once before, in the 860s, under Pope Nicholas I and Patriarch Photius.

The events of 1054 were narrower than the date suggests. Legates, meaning envoys who carry the pope's own authority, arrived from Rome under Cardinal Humbert. They and the patriarch of Constantinople, Michael Cerularius, excommunicated one another. Both excommunications named the men involved rather than their churches, and Leo IX had been dead three months by the time his legates issued theirs. Paul VI and Athenagoras revoked both in 1965.

The lasting break came in 1204, when Western crusaders sacked Constantinople and put one of their own commanders on its throne. Westerners held the city for the next fifty-seven years, through a succession of emperors, until a Greek army took it back in 1261.

The five branches

The early church

c. 30 – 451

Jesus of Nazareth was a Jewish preacher who worked in Galilee, a client territory under Herod Antipas, and in Judaea, which Rome governed directly. He gathered followers, and around AD 30 the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate had him crucified in Jerusalem.

The group held together after his death on a claim: that he had risen. His followers were Jews who kept the law and worshipped at the Temple, and who held that Jesus was the messiah Israel had been promised. For roughly twenty years they stayed a movement inside Judaism and nothing more.

The movement became a separate religion when it began admitting gentiles without requiring them to become Jews first: no circumcision, no dietary law. The apostles argued that out in the 40s and 50s, Paul carried it furthest, and it let the faith spread across the Roman Empire and past its eastern edge into Persia. The emperor Constantine made it legal in 313.

Bishops meeting at Nicaea in 325 and at Constantinople in 381 produced the Nicene Creed, the statement of belief most of the churches below still recite.

By 451 five cities ran the church: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. The city a bishop governs from is his see, and these five outranked every other. Each branch below descends from this church.

Church of the East

424 – 431

The Church of the East sat in the Persian empire, in what are now Iran and Iraq, beyond the reach of Roman rule. It governed itself from 424.

It rejected the Council of Ephesus of 431. That council had condemned Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople. Nestorius held the divine and the human in Christ far apart from each other. On his account Mary bore a man in whom God dwelt, not God himself, and so no one should call her Mother of God.

The Church of the East holds that Christ has two clearly distinct natures, one divine and one human. Its missionaries followed the Silk Road east and founded churches as far away as India and China.

Today

Assyrian Church of the East. Under 1 million adherents.

Oriental Orthodox

451

The Council of Chalcedon in 451 defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human. The Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, and Malankara churches refused that wording and have stood apart ever since.

They hold that at the incarnation the divine and the human join in Christ into a single nature, neither absorbing the other. They call that position miaphysite, from the Greek for one nature.

Since the 1970s their patriarchs have signed declarations with Rome, and their theologians have agreed statements with the Eastern Orthodox, concluding that both sides believe the same thing about Christ and that the words they used for it, rather than what they meant, divided them. Those findings changed the diagnosis and not the situation: the churches remain out of communion.

Today

Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Malankara. 50 million adherents.

Eastern Orthodox

1054, then 1204

The Greek-speaking East and the Latin-speaking West drifted apart over language and politics, and then over two disputes. The first is whether the pope holds authority over every other bishop, or is simply the most senior of them.

The second is the filioque. As the council of 381 settled it, the creed says the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father. The Western church later inserted and the Son — filioque in Latin — so that in the West the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. The East objected twice over: to the claim itself, which it read as giving the Trinity two sources rather than one, and to Rome changing the wording of a creed that a council had agreed and that no single church was free to revise.

The Orthodox churches reject the papal claim and the added words alike. Authority among them rests with councils of bishops and with self-governing national churches, each under its own patriarch.

Today

Greek, Russian, Serbian, Romanian, Antiochian. 220 million adherents.

Roman Catholic

1054 onward

The Roman Catholic Church is the Western church, and the bishop of Rome leads it. His authority is direct rather than ceremonial: the pope governs every Catholic bishop, instead of presiding over a council of equals.

It recognizes seven sacraments. It teaches purgatory, a state of purification after death for those who die in God's friendship but unready for heaven. It keeps a teaching office, the magisterium, which can settle a disputed question and bind the whole church to the answer.

Two councils made it what it is now. Trent, sitting from 1545 to 1563, was Rome's reply to the Reformation: it went through the Reformers' claims and defined Catholic doctrine against them one by one, and it required every diocese to train its priests in a seminary. The Second Vatican Council, from 1962 to 1965, let priests say the Mass in local languages instead of Latin and gave lay people a part in worship. It also reopened dealings with the churches Rome had separated from.

Today

Latin Church plus 23 Eastern Catholic churches. 1.4 billion adherents.

Protestant

1517 onward

Protestantism began with Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar who taught theology at the university of Wittenberg. In 1517 he attacked the trade in indulgences.

An indulgence was not forgiveness. Catholic teaching held then, as it holds now, that forgiveness lifts the guilt of a sin but leaves a debt of punishment to discharge, in this life or in purgatory. An indulgence cancelled part or all of that debt. The church granted them for prescribed acts: prayer, pilgrimage, and almsgiving. By 1517 the prescribed act could be a payment, and the campaign preachers were running across Germany offered indulgences for the dead as well as the living.

Luther attacked that trade, and the preaching around it, which left ordinary people believing the money had bought them forgiveness outright.

The churches that followed hold that Scripture outranks church tradition, that God accepts people through faith rather than earned merit, and that only baptism and communion count as sacraments.

Protestantism is not a single church. The word covers thousands of separate denominations, and new ones keep forming.

Today

Lutheran, Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal. 1 billion adherents.

Inside Protestantism

Protestantism is not a single church. The word covers thousands of separate denominations, and new ones keep forming.

Lutheran

1517 – 1530

Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar teaching at Wittenberg, posted 95 arguments against indulgences on October 31, 1517. He held that Scripture alone binds the conscience and that God accepts people through faith, not merit. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 set out the position. Lutheran churches keep liturgy, vestments, and the belief that Christ is truly present in the bread and wine.

Reformed

1519 – 1536

Huldrych Zwingli began reforming Zurich in 1519. John Calvin, a French lawyer living in exile in Geneva, published the Institutes of the Christian Religion in 1536. Both stripped worship back to preaching and Scripture, and took the images out of the churches.

Calvin taught predestination: God has already settled who will be saved, and nothing a person does alters it. Boards of elders govern these churches. There are no bishops.

Anglican

1534 – 1571

Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534 so that he could annul his marriage. Doctrine had nothing to do with it. Thomas Cranmer, his archbishop of Canterbury, supplied the theology through the Book of Common Prayer of 1549, which gave English worship a single form still in use. The Thirty-Nine Articles followed in 1571. Bishops and sacraments remain, and unity rests on praying together rather than on signing a doctrinal statement.

Radical Reformation (Anabaptist)

1525

In January 1525 Conrad Grebel baptized George Blaurock as an adult in Zurich, and Blaurock baptized the rest. Infant baptism is no baptism at all, they argued, because no one inherits faith. Their opponents called them Anabaptists, rebaptizers.

They held that a church is a voluntary body of believers, and that the state has no business running one. Many refused oaths and military service, which in the sixteenth century made them enemies of whatever government they lived under.

Zurich drowned Felix Manz in the Limmat, the river running through the city, in 1527. He was the first Anabaptist other Protestants put to death, and Zurich chose the method to mock him: a third baptism for a man who had insisted on a second. The imperial diet at Speyer made rebaptism a capital offence in 1529, and the killing spread through Switzerland, southern Germany, Austria, and the Low Countries, by drowning, burning, and beheading, for the next fifty years.

Two groups descend from them. The Mennonites take their name from Menno Simons, a Dutch priest who joined the movement in 1536. The Amish split from the Mennonites in 1693 under Jakob Ammann, a Swiss elder who wanted the church to shun expelled members more strictly.

Baptist

1609

John Smyth, an English separatist exiled in Amsterdam, baptized himself and his congregation as believing adults in 1609. Baptists hold that baptism follows a personal decision, that each congregation governs itself, and that no creed binds it.

Congregational and Quaker

1580s and 1652

English separatists under Robert Browne, a Cambridge-educated preacher, rejected bishops outright from the 1580s. Their descendants founded Plymouth Colony.

George Fox's Quakers, from 1652, kept neither clergy nor sacraments. They meet in silence, and whoever feels the call to speak speaks.

Methodist

1738 – 1795

John Wesley, an Anglican priest, led a revival that ran on personal holiness, lay preaching, and hymns his brother Charles largely wrote. American Methodists organized as a separate church in 1784. Wesley died in 1791, and the British followed four years after that.

Episcopal Church (United States)

1784 – 1789

After independence, American Anglicans had no bishops and no way to get any. Consecration in England required an oath of loyalty to the British crown, which they could not swear.

Samuel Seabury went to the Scottish Episcopal Church instead, whose bishops demanded no such oath, and they consecrated him at Aberdeen in 1784. The church adopted a constitution and its own prayer book at the 1789 General Convention.

Adventist and Restorationist

1832 – 1863

These movements set aside the later history of the church and tried to rebuild the first-century one from Scripture alone.

Barton Stone and Alexander Campbell were American preachers who had reached that conclusion separately, and their followers merged in 1832. Seventh-day Adventists grew out of the Millerites, who had expected Christ to return in 1844. They organized as a church in 1863, and keep Saturday as the Sabbath.

Pentecostal

1901 – 1906

Pentecostalism began at a small Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, in 1901, where students started speaking in tongues. It became a worldwide movement through the revival the Holiness preacher William Seymour led on Azusa Street in Los Angeles from 1906.

Pentecostals teach that a second experience follows conversion, baptism in the Holy Spirit. Speaking in tongues is its sign, and healing is among the gifts it brings.

Latter-day Saints, founded in 1830, and Jehovah's Witnesses, organized from the 1870s, are usually charted apart from Protestantism. Both reject the doctrine of the Trinity set out at Nicaea, which every branch above shares.

The seven sacraments are baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, ordination, and marriage. Most Protestant churches count only baptism and the Eucharist, on the grounds that Scripture records Jesus instituting those two.

Key dates

325

Council of Nicaea

Condemns Arius, a senior priest in Alexandria, who taught that the Son was created by the Father rather than eternal with him.
381

Council of Constantinople

Adds the section on the Holy Spirit and completes the creed still recited in most of these churches.
431

Council of Ephesus

Condemns Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, over how the divine and human join in Christ. The Church of the East does not follow.
451

Council of Chalcedon

Defines Christ as one person in two natures. The Oriental Orthodox reject the wording and separate.
787

Second Council of Nicaea

Ends the first campaign against religious images. The last council both East and West accept.
1054

Mutual excommunications

Envoys of Pope Leo IX and Patriarch Michael Cerularius excommunicate each other in Constantinople.
1204

Sack of Constantinople

Crusaders bound for Egypt take the city instead and install a Latin ruler for nearly sixty years.
1378–1417

Western Schism

Rival popes in Rome and Avignon claim the office at once, discrediting the papacy and inviting reform.
1517

Luther's 95 Theses

Academic objections to the sale of indulgences circulate in print and become a rupture within months.
1525

Zurich adult baptisms

Adults rebaptize one another in Zurich, beginning the Radical Reformation and its rejection of state churches.
1534

Act of Supremacy

Parliament makes Henry VIII head of the English church, ending papal jurisdiction in England.
1545–1563

Council of Trent

Rome defines its doctrine against the Reformers and overhauls the training and conduct of clergy.
1549

Book of Common Prayer

Thomas Cranmer, archbishop of Canterbury, gives English worship one vernacular form, still the basis of Anglican liturgy.
1609

First Baptist congregation

John Smyth baptizes believing adults in Amsterdam and founds the first Baptist congregation.
1738

Wesley's conversion

John Wesley converts at a London meeting and begins the Methodist movement inside Anglicanism.
1789

Episcopal Church constituted

American Anglicans adopt a constitution and their own prayer book after independence.
1870

First Vatican Council

Defines that the pope teaches without error when ruling formally on faith and morals.
1906

Azusa Street revival

William Seymour, a Holiness preacher, leads a revival in Los Angeles that turns Pentecostalism into a global movement.
1962–1965

Second Vatican Council

Mass in local languages, openness to other churches, and a larger role for lay people.
1965

Excommunications lifted

Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras revoke the mutual condemnations of 1054.
