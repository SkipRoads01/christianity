# The tic catalogue

Read this when revising prose that feels wrong for reasons you cannot name, or
when checking someone else's draft. Each entry gives the fault, why it is a
fault, and a repair.

## 1. Constructions that hide the actor

**Pseudo-cleft.** `What held the group together was a claim.` The sentence takes
a plain statement and wraps it so an abstraction can be the grammatical subject.
It sounds considered. It is scaffolding.
Repair: `The group held together on a claim.`

**Agentless passive.** `Communion has not been restored.` Somebody did or did not
do this.
Repair: `The churches have not restored communion.`

**Passive with the actor demoted to a by-phrase.** `He was put to death by other
Protestants.`
Repair: `Other Protestants put him to death.`

**Nominalization as subject.** `Seymour's revival turns Pentecostalism into a
global movement.` A noun made from a verb, doing the work a person should do.
Repair: `Seymour leads a revival that turns Pentecostalism into a global
movement.`

**Abstract noun plus linking verb.** `A claim to kingship is sedition.` The verb
is `is`, which means the sentence has no action in it.
Repair: name the actor and the act.

**Subjectless fragment.** `Became the standard formula.` `Rejected by every
Reformation tradition.` Common in list entries and captions, where it reads as
clipped rather than economical.
Repair: `It became the standard formula.` `Every Reformation tradition rejected
it.`

**Dangling or floating participle.** `..., offered as a compromise to win the
provinces back.` Attached to nothing in particular.
Repair: `..., offering it as a compromise to win the provinces back.`

**Reduced relative clause used as padding.** `The charge fixed to the cross names
him king of the Jews.` The clause buries the point of the sentence.
Repair: `The sign over the cross names the charge.`

## 2. Constructions that perform thought without doing any

**Not X, but Y.** `It's not a disagreement, it's a failure.` Promises a
distinction, delivers emphasis. The most reliable single marker of machine
prose.
Repair: say the thing once, in its own sentence.

**Not just X, but Y.** As above, with a bonus intensifier.

**Not only X but also Y.** As above, more formal.

**The rule of three.** `Clear, concise, and compelling.` `Money, buildings, and
legal authority.` Three items feel complete, so three items appear whether or not
there are three. Sometimes there really are three; count them and be sure.

**Rhetorical question as transition.** `So what changed?` Write the transition.

**The faux-profound one-line paragraph.** A single short sentence given its own
paragraph for weight it has not earned.

**Antithesis for its own sake.** `The words divided them; the meaning united
them.` Check that both halves are true before enjoying the symmetry.

**Summarising coda.** The last sentence of a paragraph restating the paragraph.
Delete it and see whether anything is lost. Usually nothing is.

**Signposting what you are about to do.** `Let me walk you through this.`
`Here's what I found.` Then do it.

## 3. Rhythm faults

**The staccato cascade.** Five or more short declaratives in a row, all the same
shape. Produced by over-applying "give every sentence an acting subject." The
repair is fewer sentences with subordination, not more sentences.

**Uniform paragraph length.** Every paragraph three sentences long. Real prose
has paragraphs of one sentence and paragraphs of seven.

**Anaphora by accident.** Three or four consecutive sentences opening on the same
word. `They regroup... they say... they have seen... They stay.` Fine as a
deliberate figure, a defect when unnoticed.

**Em dash as a drum roll.** One or two per page. More than that and every
sentence has a dramatic pause in it.

**Colon used as a drum roll.** Same fault. A colon introduces a list or an
explanation, not suspense.

## 4. Vocabulary

Never use these. The plain alternative follows.

| Avoid | Use |
| --- | --- |
| delve into | examine, look at |
| tapestry, mosaic (figurative) | cut the metaphor |
| a testament to | shows, proves |
| landscape, realm, sphere (figurative) | the actual subject |
| navigate (figurative) | handle, work through |
| leverage (verb) | use |
| utilize | use |
| robust | strong, reliable, or say how |
| seamless, frictionless | say what does not break |
| crucial, pivotal, vital | important, or say why |
| underscore, highlight (figurative) | show, stress |
| harness (figurative) | use |
| unlock, elevate, foster | say the plain verb |
| myriad, plethora | many, or the number |
| boasts | has |
| nestled | is, sits |
| dive in, deep dive, unpack | start, examine |
| at its core, in essence, fundamentally | cut |
| game-changer, transformative | say what changed |
| rich history, storied past | the actual history |

Intensifiers to cut on sight: incredibly, truly, genuinely, really, very,
absolutely, quite, extremely. If the sentence needs them, the noun or verb is
wrong.

## 5. Padding and hedging

Cut these phrases entirely:

- It's important to note that
- It's worth mentioning that
- It should be noted that
- That being said / With that said
- At the end of the day
- In today's fast-paced world
- When it comes to
- The fact of the matter is
- Needless to say
- In order to (use "to")

**Hedge stacking.** `may potentially`, `could possibly`, `might perhaps`. One
hedge or none.

**Weasel attribution.** `Many experts believe`, `studies show`, `it is widely
regarded`. Name the person or the study, or drop the claim. On this site,
naming is mandatory.

**False precision as hedge.** `roughly approximately`, `about 49 or so`.

**Apologising for the content.** `This is a complex topic, but...` Write the
sentence.

## 6. Structure abuse

**Headings on a short answer.** Two paragraphs do not need a heading, and three
sentences do not need three headings.

**Tables for two facts.** A table earns its place when there are rows to compare.

**Bold lead-ins on every bullet.** When everything is emphasised, nothing is.

**Numbered list for prose.** Numbers imply sequence or count. If neither applies,
write paragraphs.

**Nested lists past two levels.** Rewrite the content instead.

**Emoji, exclamation marks, and section dividers as decoration.** None of these.

## 7. Conversational tics

**Sycophantic opener.** `Great question`, `Good catch`, `You're absolutely
right`, `Excellent point`. Show agreement by acting on it.

**Restating the question before answering.** `You're asking whether the deploy
failed.` Answer it.

**The trailing offer, every turn.** `Want me to also...?` `Say the word and
I'll...` Occasionally useful, relentless when habitual. Offer when there is a
specific, non-obvious next step and you have reason to think it is wanted.

**Correction theater.** Narrating your own error at length, tallying past errors,
grading your own performance, apologising more than once. It reads as
performance, and it buries the correction. Fix it, state what changed in one
sentence, continue.

**Fragment-quoting.** Referring to `the other two` or `that sentence` without
quoting them. The reader does not have your context loaded.

**Hedged verdicts on verified facts.** If the test passed, it passed. `It seems
to be working` about something you watched work is false modesty.

**Closing pleasantry.** `I hope this helps.` `Let me know if you have any
questions.` Cut.

## 8. Faults specific to writing history

**Generalising a specific event.** `Rome crucifies men who claim a throne` turns
one execution into a standing practice and moves the subject off the man being
executed. Write about the event you are describing.

**Unnamed agents.** `A group baptized one another.` `Bishops met.` Name them:
Conrad Grebel, George Blaurock. If the name is unknown, say so or cut the
sentence.

**Ambiguous titles.** `Caesar` in AD 30 means Tiberius, not Julius, who died in
44 BC. Any title that changed hands needs the holder named.

**Flattening a distinction to save words.** `Galilee and Judaea, both under Roman
rule` collapses a client tetrarchy and a directly governed province. If the
distinction matters later in the passage, make it; if it does not, do not raise
the subject.

**Category error from compression.** `A man who calls himself king is sedition.`
A man is not a crime. The claim is.

**Anachronistic framing.** Describing a first-century movement in the vocabulary
of modern institutions, or vice versa.

**Tense drift.** This site narrates in the historical present. A past-tense verb
inside a present-tense passage stands out. The exception is a verb inside a
reported claim: `Arius taught that the Son was created` is correct, because the
creation is prior to the teaching.
