Section one, walked forward

Two thousand years of branching

Read an era, then move the year forward. The chart above draws itself as you go, and each branch appears at the moment it leaves.

Fourteen steps from AD 30 to today. Everything here also appears, all at once and in reference form, on the denominations page.

c. AD 30 1 of 14

The movement begins

Christianity begins as a movement inside Judaism among followers of Jesus of Nazareth, executed in Jerusalem around AD 30. There is one church, and no word yet for anything else.

It spreads across the Roman Empire and past its eastern edge, into Persia and beyond. Every church on this timeline claims descent from this one body.

313 – 325 2 of 14

Constantine, then Nicaea

The emperor Constantine legalizes Christianity in 313. Twelve years later bishops meet at Nicaea and condemn Arius, who taught that the Son was created by the Father rather than eternal with him.

The council settles on a single word, homoousios, of the same substance. Its rival differed by one letter.

381 3 of 14

The creed is finished

A second council at Constantinople adds the section on the Holy Spirit and completes the creed still recited in most of the churches on this page.

Around the same time the Cappadocians solve a vocabulary problem that had made the Trinity impossible to state: ousia for what God is, of which there is one, and hypostasis for who God is, of which there are three.

431 4 of 14

Ephesus, and the first parting

The Council of Ephesus condemns Nestorius, the patriarch of Constantinople, for so separating the divine and the human in Christ that Mary should not be called Mother of God.

The church centered in Persia, outside Roman rule and self-governing since 424, does not follow. It holds that Christ has two clearly distinct natures, one divine and one human. Missionaries carry it along the Silk Road to India and China. It is the first branch to leave, and it never returns.

451 5 of 14

Chalcedon

Chalcedon defines Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human. A group of churches rejects the wording. They hold that the divine and human join into one united nature, a position called miaphysite.

The Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, and Malankara churches descend from this refusal. Joint statements since the 1970s conclude that the two sides mean much the same thing in different vocabularies, but communion has not been restored.

787 – 1204 6 of 14

East and West

The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 ends the first campaign against religious images. It is the last council both East and West accept.

After it the Greek-speaking East and the Latin-speaking West drift apart over language, politics, and two questions: whether the pope holds authority over all other bishops, and whether the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone. In 1054 envoys of Pope Leo IX and Patriarch Michael Cerularius excommunicate each other.

The date is a convenience for a separation that took centuries. The sack of Constantinople by Western crusaders in 1204 did more lasting damage than the paperwork did.

1378 – 1417 7 of 14

The Western Schism

Rival popes in Rome and Avignon claim the office at once. The spectacle discredits the papacy and makes the case for reform to people who had no intention of leaving the church.

Nothing splits yet. The ground is being prepared.

1517 8 of 14

Luther

Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar teaching at Wittenberg, posts 95 arguments against the sale of indulgences on October 31, 1517. Printing turns an academic objection into a rupture within months.

The churches that follow hold that Scripture outranks church tradition, that God accepts people through faith rather than earned merit, and that only baptism and communion count as sacraments. This branch is unlike the others: it will not stop dividing.

1525 – 1534 9 of 14

The Reformation multiplies

Zurich, January 1525: a group baptizes one another as adults, arguing that faith cannot be inherited. Catholic and Protestant authorities execute them in large numbers. Their descendants are the Mennonites and the Amish.

Marburg, 1529: Luther and Zwingli agree on fourteen of fifteen articles and break on the fifteenth, over whether is in “this is my body” means is or signifies. Lutheran and Reformed separate over one verb.

London, 1534: Parliament makes Henry VIII head of the English church, to obtain an annulment rather than over doctrine. Thomas Cranmer supplies the theology afterward.

1545 – 1563 10 of 14

Rome answers

The Council of Trent defines Roman doctrine against the Reformers and overhauls the training and conduct of the clergy. It affirms transubstantiation as the apt term in 1551.

The lines are now drawn where they will stay for four hundred years.

1609 – 1789 11 of 14

Separatists, Quakers, Methodists

John Smyth baptizes believing adults in Amsterdam in 1609, giving believer's baptism organized form. George Fox's Quakers, from 1652, drop clergy and sacraments altogether and meet in silence.

John Wesley's experience at a London meeting in 1738 begins a revival inside Anglicanism built on personal holiness, lay preaching, and hymns. It leaves the Church of England after his death.

After American independence, Anglicans who can no longer swear loyalty to the crown constitute the Episcopal Church in 1789.

1906 12 of 14

Azusa Street

William Seymour's revival in Los Angeles turns Pentecostalism into a global movement. It teaches a second experience after conversion, baptism in the Holy Spirit, marked by speaking in tongues and by healing.

It came through the Holiness movement, which was Methodist, which was Anglican, which was Roman. Every branch on this chart has branches of its own.

1962 – 1965 13 of 14

The turn outward

The Second Vatican Council opens Roman worship to local languages and to lay participation, and opens the church to conversation with everyone it had separated from.

In 1965 Paul VI and Athenagoras revoke the mutual condemnations of 1054, nine hundred and eleven years after they were issued. The separation remains.

Today 14 of 14

Where it stands

Five families, all claiming the same descent. Roman Catholic, 1.3 to 1.4 billion. Protestant, about 1 billion across thousands of bodies. Eastern Orthodox, about 220 million. Oriental Orthodox, about 50 million. The Assyrian Church of the East, under 1 million.

None of the splits since 431 has been undone. Several have been declared misunderstandings by both sides, and remain in force anyway.

That is the whole timeline.
